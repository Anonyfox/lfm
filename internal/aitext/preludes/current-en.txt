Chapter Three: Choosing Plants That Forgive the Dark

If your flat faces north, sits below street level, or looks out onto a brick wall three metres away, you have probably already lost a plant or two. Most people blame themselves, but the problem usually starts at the garden centre. Many of the plants sold as "easy" assume a bright windowsill. In a dim room they slowly starve, and the decline is so gradual that you only notice once it is too late.

The first thing to understand is that light is food. A plant in a dark corner is not resting. It is running on a deficit, using up stored energy faster than it can make more. That is why low-light plants are not plants that like the dark. They are plants that can survive on very little, and survival depends on you not asking them to do more than they can.

Start by measuring what you actually have. You do not need special equipment. Around midday, hold your hand about thirty centimetres above the spot where you want the plant to live. If your hand casts a crisp, defined shadow, you have bright light. A soft shadow with blurred edges means medium light. If you can barely see a shadow at all, you are in low light, and you should be honest with yourself about that. A free light meter app on your phone can give you a rough number if you prefer, and anything below about 500 lux for most of the day counts as low.

For genuinely low light, a few plants stand out. The ZZ plant stores water in thick underground rhizomes and can go weeks without attention. The snake plant, now officially called Dracaena trifasciata, tolerates dim rooms and irregular watering better than almost anything else. Pothos will grow in low light, though it will become leggy and lose some of its variegation. Cast iron plant earned its name in Victorian parlours lit by gas lamps. Peace lilies will survive, although they rarely flower without more light.

The most important adjustment you can make is to water less. A plant in low light uses water slowly, so soil stays wet for much longer than it would on a sunny windowsill. Overwatering is the most common way dark-flat plants die. Before you water, push a finger into the soil up to the second knuckle. If it feels at all damp, wait. For ZZ plants and snake plants, wait until the pot feels noticeably lighter when you lift it.

Use a pot with a drainage hole. A decorative outer pot is fine, but the plant itself should sit in a plastic nursery pot that lets excess water escape. Tip out any water that collects at the bottom ten minutes after watering.

Finally, move the plant as close to your best window as you reasonably can. Even a metre makes a surprising difference, because light drops off sharply with distance. If that still is not enough, a single inexpensive LED grow bulb in an ordinary lamp, left on for ten to twelve hours a day, can turn a struggling plant into a thriving one.

How I Finally Learned to Cook Rice Properly

For years, rice was the one thing I could not get right. I could roast a chicken, make a decent risotto, and bake bread that people actually asked for, but a simple pot of plain white rice defeated me nearly every time. It was either crunchy in the middle, gluey on the bottom, or both at once, which I still do not fully understand.

If that sounds familiar, I want to share what finally worked for me. None of it is complicated. It just took me an embarrassingly long time to stop ignoring it.

The first thing I changed was rinsing. I used to skip this step because it felt fussy. Then a friend made me watch as she rinsed a cup of jasmine rice under the tap, swirling it with her fingers, until the cloudy water ran almost clear. That cloudiness is surface starch, and it is what turns your rice sticky and clumped. Rinsing takes about a minute. Do it in a sieve or in the pot itself, changing the water three or four times. It makes a real difference.

The second thing was measuring the water properly. The ratio printed on most packets is often too much. For long-grain white rice like jasmine or basmati, I now use about one and a quarter cups of water for every cup of rinsed rice. Short-grain sushi rice needs slightly more, and brown rice needs much more, closer to two cups, plus a longer cooking time. Your stove and your pot will matter, so treat these as starting points and adjust.

The third change was the one that really fixed things: I stopped lifting the lid. Rice cooks mostly by steam, not by boiling. Every time you peek, you let that steam escape and the timing falls apart. Here is my method now. Put the rinsed rice and water in a heavy pot with a pinch of salt. Bring it to a boil uncovered. As soon as it boils, stir once, put the lid on, and turn the heat down as low as it will go. Set a timer for twelve minutes for white rice. Do not touch it.

When the timer goes off, turn off the heat and leave the lid on for another ten minutes. This resting time is not optional. It lets the moisture redistribute so the grains at the top finish cooking and the grains at the bottom loosen from the pan. Then take the lid off and fluff the rice gently with a fork.

That is it. Rinse, measure, simmer covered, rest, fluff.

A few smaller things helped too. A pot with a tight-fitting lid works far better than one that rattles. If your lid is loose, put a clean tea towel under it. And if you cook rice more than twice a week, a basic rice cooker is honestly worth the counter space.

It sounds silly to feel proud of a pot of rice, but the first time every grain came out separate and tender, I did a small victory lap around the kitchen. I hope you get to do the same.

Preparing for the Interview

Most people prepare for a job interview by trying to predict the questions. That is useful, but it is only part of the work. The candidates who perform best tend to prepare in three areas: understanding the role, understanding themselves, and handling the practical details that can undermine an otherwise strong performance.

Start with the role. Read the job description again, slowly, and highlight every responsibility and requirement. Then ask yourself what problem this company is trying to solve by hiring someone. A job advert is rarely just a list of tasks. It usually reflects a gap in the team, a new project, or a pain point that someone senior wants fixed. If you can identify that underlying need, you can shape your answers around it.

Next, research the organisation. Look at its website, recent news, annual reports if it publishes them, and the LinkedIn profiles of the people who will interview you. You are not trying to memorise facts to recite. You are trying to understand the context well enough to ask good questions and to connect your experience to what matters to them.

Then turn to yourself. For each key requirement in the job description, find one or two examples from your past work that show you can do it. A helpful structure for these examples is situation, task, action and result. Briefly describe the context, explain what you were responsible for, focus most of your time on what you specifically did, and finish with the outcome, ideally with a number or a concrete change. Write these stories down and practise saying them aloud. They will feel awkward at first, and that is exactly why rehearsal matters.

Prepare answers for the questions that come up in nearly every interview. Why do you want this job? Why are you leaving your current role? What is your greatest strength? Tell me about a time something went wrong. Keep your answers honest and specific. Interviewers have heard every generic response many times, and a real example will always be more memorable than a polished cliche.

Prepare your own questions too. Aim for three or four, and make them genuine. Ask what success looks like in the first six months, what the biggest challenges facing the team are, or how the role has changed over time. Avoid asking anything you could easily have found on the website.

Finally, take care of the practical details. Confirm the time, the format and the names of the people you will meet. If the interview is in person, plan your route and aim to arrive ten minutes early. If it is online, test your camera, microphone and internet connection the day before, and choose a quiet spot with a plain background. Lay out your clothes the night before and have a printed copy of your CV to hand.

Preparation will not remove nerves entirely, and it should not. A little adrenaline keeps you sharp. But it will give you something solid to stand on when the questions begin.

How to Clean a Cast-Iron Pan

Cast-iron pans have a reputation for being difficult to look after, but the truth is that they are very forgiving. With a few simple habits, a cast-iron pan will last for generations and only get better the more you use it. The key is understanding that you are not just cleaning the pan. You are protecting its seasoning.

Seasoning is the thin layer of polymerised oil that has bonded to the surface of the iron. It gives the pan its dark colour, makes it naturally non-stick, and protects it from rust. Good cleaning keeps that layer intact. Here is how to do it.

What you will need

Hot water, a stiff brush or non-scratch scrubber, a clean tea towel or paper towels, and a neutral cooking oil such as vegetable, sunflower or rapeseed. Coarse salt and a chainmail scrubber are useful for stubborn residue.

Step one: clean it while it is still warm

The easiest time to clean cast iron is shortly after cooking, while the pan is still warm but cool enough to handle safely. Food comes away much more easily before it has had time to harden.

Step two: rinse and scrub

Run the pan under hot water and scrub it with your brush. For most everyday cooking, this is all you need. If something has stuck, pour in a tablespoon of coarse salt with a splash of water and use it as an abrasive paste. For really stubborn bits, add a little water to the pan, bring it to a simmer on the stove for a minute or two, and then scrape gently with a wooden spatula.

A note on soap

You may have heard that soap should never touch cast iron. That advice dates from a time when soaps contained lye, which could strip seasoning. Modern washing-up liquid is much milder, and a small amount will not harm a well-seasoned pan. Just avoid soaking the pan or putting it in the dishwasher.

Step three: dry it completely

This is the most important step. Water is the enemy of cast iron, and even a small amount left on the surface can cause rust overnight. Wipe the pan thoroughly with a towel, then place it on the stove over low heat for a few minutes until every trace of moisture has evaporated.

Step four: oil it lightly

While the pan is still warm, add about half a teaspoon of oil and rub it all over the cooking surface with a paper towel. Then take a clean paper towel and wipe away as much as you can. It should look dry, not shiny or greasy. Too much oil will turn sticky.

Step five: store it somewhere dry

Keep the pan in a dry cupboard or on the stovetop. If you stack other pans inside it, put a paper towel in between to protect the surface and absorb any moisture.

What to do about rust

If you do find rust, do not panic. Scrub the affected area with steel wool until you reach bare metal, wash and dry the pan, then coat it with a thin layer of oil and bake it upside down in a 230 degree Celsius oven for an hour. It will be as good as new.


A houseplant does not need a perfect home or a perfectly attentive owner. It needs a little observation and a routine that suits its kind. Before you bring a plant home, think about the light in the spot where it will live. A sunny windowsill, a bright room away from the glass, and a dim corner are very different places. A plant that loves direct sun may struggle in a dark hallway, while delicate leaves can scorch in strong afternoon light.

Start by learning the plant’s name and its basic needs. Keep the nursery label, or write the name somewhere you can find it later. Check the soil before watering: press a finger about two centimeters into the mix. If it feels dry, water slowly until a little runs from the drainage holes. Empty the saucer so the roots are not left sitting in water. If the soil is still damp, wait and check again in a few days. A fixed watering schedule can be tempting, but a plant’s needs change with the season, the temperature, and the amount of light it receives.

Choose a pot with drainage, and use a potting mix suited to the plant. If leaves turn yellow or stems become soft, check the soil and roots before reaching for fertilizer. Too much water is a common cause of trouble. Brown, crispy leaf edges can have several causes, including dry air, inconsistent watering, or mineral buildup, so look at the plant’s conditions as a whole. Remove dead leaves with clean scissors, turn the pot now and then so growth stays balanced, and give yourself time to notice what changes. A thriving plant is not one that never loses a leaf. It is one whose needs you are learning to meet.

Cooking rice is a small kitchen skill with a large payoff. The key is to match the method to the rice. For everyday long-grain white rice, begin with one cup of rice and about one and a half cups of water. Rinse the rice in a bowl or fine sieve, swirling it until the water looks less cloudy. This removes loose surface starch and helps the grains cook more separately.

Put the rinsed rice, water, and a pinch of salt in a saucepan. Bring it to a boil, then cover the pan and turn the heat to its lowest setting. Let it cook gently for about fifteen minutes. Resist the urge to lift the lid; the trapped steam is doing important work. When the time is up, turn off the heat and leave the pan covered for another ten minutes. Fluff the rice gently with a fork.

Different varieties need different amounts of water and time. Brown rice usually takes longer and needs more water, while short-grain rice is naturally stickier. Check the package for a starting point, then adjust next time to suit your stove and preferred texture. If the rice is still firm when the water is gone, add a small splash of hot water, cover it, and cook a little longer. If it is wet, let it stand uncovered briefly after cooking. Once you know your pot, your rice, and your burner, a dependable bowl is easy to make.
